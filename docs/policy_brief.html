Policy Brief

Electricity-Dependent Disability and Climate Hazard Convergence:
A County-Level Risk Analysis

Informing Rehabilitation Preparedness Policy in an Era of Climate Change
Barrett Jackson | Alexander Lab, Marcus Neuroscience Institute April 2026

The Problem

People with spinal cord injuries and other physical disabilities depend on electricity-powered medical equipment — ventilators, oxygen concentrators, powered wheelchairs, suction, IV infusion — for survival or essential function. The hazard is not the disability; it is the failure of power infrastructure to keep flowing to people whose bodies depend on it. Climate-driven extreme weather is the primary modern driver of large-scale grid disruption, yet current disaster preparedness frameworks do not systematically map where electricity-dependent populations and climate hazard converge.

Population Definition

The operational denominator throughout this brief is electricity-dependent Medicare beneficiaries — the HHS emPOWER cohort with claims for survival-critical powered durable medical equipment (ventilators, oxygen concentrators, powered wheelchairs, suction, IV infusion). Spinal cord injury is the grant's clinical motivation but cannot be isolated from public Medicare files; SCI-specific conclusions are triangulated through the parallel clinical study arm rather than read directly off this map.

The Analysis

This analysis joins the FEMA National Risk Index and the HHS emPOWER Map at the county level across 3,131 scored US counties plus 78 Puerto Rico municipios. Each county receives a national hazard percentile (FEMA NRI 5-hazard composite: hurricane, heat wave, wildfire, coastal flood, drought) and a national power-dependency percentile (electricity-dependent Medicare beneficiaries per total Medicare beneficiary). The vulnerability index is the equal-weighted mean of the two percentiles — a dual-percentile rescaling that prevents either axis from dominating the index by raw scale. Convergent vulnerability is treated throughout as a property of the infrastructure-population system, not of individuals.

Key Findings

The national top-25 for convergent vulnerability is dominated by an Intermountain West cluster (high power-dependency, moderate-to-high hazard); the South Florida coast carries the largest absolute electricity-dependent counts at the highest hazard percentiles; Puerto Rico is invisible to the federal hazard framework despite documented hurricane exposure.

  • Two structural typologies. Intermountain West counties (San Juan NM 96.9, El Paso CO 94.5, Bernalillo NM 94.2, Mesa CO 92.9, Utah UT 93.3) reach the top of the index via concentrated electricity-dependent populations paired with regional drought, wildfire, and heat-wave exposure. The South Florida coast (Palm Beach FL 99.8, Miami-Dade FL 99.7, Broward FL 98.1) reaches the top via top-1% hurricane and coastal-flood hazard with large absolute electricity-dependent counts.
  • Puerto Rico is a hazard-data gap, not a low-risk zone. All 78 PR municipios are marked Insufficient Data in FEMA NRI; the territory carries an estimated 49,000 electricity-dependent Medicare beneficiaries and lived experience of multi-week power outages from Hurricanes Maria (2017), Fiona (2022), and Ernesto (2024). The data gap is itself a finding worth naming.
  • Rate vs. count diverge. Counties in the top decile by power-dependency rate are predominantly rural and Intermountain West; counties in the top decile by absolute electricity-dependent count are predominantly populous and coastal. Preparedness planning needs both views.

Statistical Findings

  • Spearman ρ = −0.218 (95% CI −0.250 … −0.181; p < 1e-30; n = 3,099 CONUS counties). The hazard and power-dependency axes are statistically near-independent — and slightly inversely structured — at the county level. The two-typology framing is recovered as one coefficient.
  • Global Moran's I (Queen contiguity, 999 permutations): vulnerability index I = +0.707; hazard percentile I = +0.670; power-dependency percentile I = +0.748. All p < 0.001 — regional clustering is real, not a visual artifact.
  • LISA cluster classification (p < 0.05): 676 high-high counties (convergent vulnerability bloc), 578 low-low counties, 16 high-VI outliers, 27 low-VI outliers. The HH count is the formal answer to "how many counties make up the convergent-vulnerability cluster?"
  • Attributable burden: 793,431 electricity-dependent Medicare beneficiaries (26.8% of the national total) live in counties at or above the 90th percentile of FEMA hazard; 503,131 (17.0%) above P95; 148,276 (5.0%) above P99. Risk ratios versus a population-proportional counterfactual hover near 0.9 — the population is not generally over-concentrated in high-hazard counties; convergent risk is locational, concentrated in the LISA high-high bloc.
  • Spatial-error regression of VI on ACS covariates plus state fixed effects (n = 3,098, OLS R² = 0.581, spatial pseudo-R² = 0.548): log median household income is the dominant negative predictor (β = −33.0, p < 0.001); percent uninsured is positive and significant (β = +0.22, p < 0.001); percent age 65+ and percent below poverty are not significant after controlling for income; spatial autoregressive parameter λ = +0.604. Higher-income counties carry meaningfully lower convergent vulnerability conditional on FEMA hazard, regional context, and Medicare-population scale.

Policy Implications

These findings support targeted telerehabilitation and power-redundancy infrastructure investment in the highest-vulnerability counties as a climate adaptation strategy, consistent with recommendations in Alexander & Alexander (2025). Counties in the top vulnerability quartile — and PR municipios on the absolute-count axis — should be prioritized for preparedness protocols designed around the survival-critical electricity dependency the data actually measures, not generic "vulnerable population" framings. Phase 2 will extend the framework internationally using WHO rehabilitation access data and IPCC regional climate projections.

Limitations

  • Medicare undercount. Working-age people with disabilities holding employer coverage, Medicaid-only enrollment, or no insurance are not captured in this denominator. The 24-month SSDI waiting period further excludes recently disabled individuals from Medicare-disability rates entirely.
  • Disparity propagation. Racial and geographic disparities in SSDI approval rates flow into the Medicare-disability denominator; low rates in young, low-income, rural counties may reflect access gaps rather than underlying prevalence.
  • SCI is not isolatable. Identifying the spinal-cord-injury cohort requires a CMS Data Use Agreement (ResDAC) for claims-level files; that work is out of scope for Part 1. Triangulation with the parallel clinical study arm is the path back to SCI-specific conclusions.
  • Cross-sectional only. The index does not measure behavioral adaptation, recovery, or coping. It maps where convergent risk is structurally located, not how individuals or systems respond when an event occurs.
  • Puerto Rico hazard gap. FEMA NRI returns Insufficient Data for all 78 PR municipios. PR is shown on the power-dependency axis only and excluded from the convergent vulnerability index.

Next Steps

  • International extension using WHO rehabilitation data and World Bank electricity access indicators to identify high-risk regions globally.
  • Qualitative interviews with SCI clinicians in top-decile counties to characterize existing emergency preparedness protocols and gaps.
  • Integration of NOAA historical power-outage duration data to weight the vulnerability index by realized infrastructure fragility.
  • ResDAC pull for SCI-specific cohort identification, contingent on Data Use Agreement approval.
  • Submission of findings to the Journal of Spinal Cord Medicine and the Journal of Climate Change and Health.
